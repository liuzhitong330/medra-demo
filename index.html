AI-Assisted Protein Characterization Workflow Analysis

Cathy Liu  ·  June 2026  ·  Protein Science Lab Automation Claude API SPR / DSF

Medra's Physical AI Scientist platform closes a loop that wet lab science has never fully automated: instrument operation, result interpretation, and experimental re-design — all without a human in the middle. As someone who has spent years at the bench doing exactly the work that platform is built to automate, I wanted to explore what that loop looks like from the inside: which decision points in a protein characterization campaign are actually ripe for autonomous AI reasoning, and how well can current AI handle the judgment calls that scientists make every day?

Below are two experiments I ran using the Claude API, plus a mini analysis recreating SPR and DSF data that reflects the kind of characterization work central to Medra's pipeline.

Experiment 1 — Mapping Autonomous Decision Points in a Protein Characterization Pipeline

I gave Claude a standard six-step protein characterization workflow (expression screen → purification → QC → biophysical characterization → developability → cell-based assay) and asked it to identify which three steps are most amenable to fully closed-loop AI decision-making — where a human doesn't need to review each result before the platform acts.

Prompt:

You are an expert in protein science and laboratory automation. I'm designing an autonomous protein characterization pipeline for a Physical AI Scientist platform that can operate instruments with human-level dexterity and close experimental loops without human intervention. Here is a typical protein characterization workflow: 1. Small-scale expression screen (4 constructs × 3 conditions, ExpiCHO mammalian system) 2. Protein purification (IMAC + SEC-HPLC) 3. QC analysis (SDS-PAGE, SEC-MALS, NanoDrop yield quantification) 4. Biophysical characterization (DSF thermal stability, BLI/SPR binding kinetics) 5. Developability assessment (HIC, AC-SINS, PSR) 6. Cell-based binding assay (flow cytometry on Expi293 cells expressing target) Which 3 steps would benefit most from AI-driven autonomous decision-making — where the AI scientist could close the loop without human intervention? For each, describe (a) what decision the AI makes, (b) what quantitative thresholds or data it needs, and (c) what action it triggers next.

Claude's response (28.3 s · claude-opus-4-8):

## Step 1: Expression Screen → Construct/Condition Down-Selection Why this step: This is the highest-leverage decision point. A bad call here propagates downstream and wastes weeks of automated resources. It's also a clean optimization problem with measurable outputs. (a) Decision: Which construct × condition combinations advance to purification, and whether to trigger a re-screen with adjusted parameters (e.g., temperature shift, feed timing, DNA:PEI ratio). (b) Quantitative thresholds needed: - Titer from small-scale (Octet/ProA quantitation) — threshold ≥10–50 mg/L depending on program class - Expression rank-ordering across the 12 conditions with CV across replicates <15–20% to trust a ranking - A predicted yield-after-purification estimate, since high crude titer ≠ high recoverable monomer - Historical priors: model-based expectation for this construct family (the closed loop should weight Bayesian priors from prior runs) (c) Action triggered: - If ≥1 construct clears threshold → advance top 1–2 to full purification - If all fail but trends suggest a fixable issue → autonomously launch a second optimization screen with adjusted DOE - If failure looks intrinsic (no expression across all conditions) → flag construct and escalate to human only here --- ## Step 2: SEC-HPLC Purification → Fractionation & Polishing Decision Why this step: Chromatograms are exactly the kind of structured, real-time signal where automated peak interpretation outperforms waiting for human review. Decisions must be made during the run (seconds matter for fraction collection). (a) Decision: Which SEC fractions to pool, whether monomer purity warrants a polishing re-run, and whether to switch elution/buffer strategy. (b) Thresholds needed: - % monomer by SEC peak integration (pool only fractions contributing to ≥95–98% monomer) - Aggregate (HMW) and fragment (LMW) percentages - Peak resolution (Rs) between monomer and adjacent species - Carryover yield budget: minimum recoverable mass to justify proceeding downstream (c) Action triggered: - Clean monomer peak above purity floor → auto-pool, concentrate, advance to QC - HMW >5–10% → trigger autonomous re-injection on polishing SEC or add HIC/IEX step - Yield below downstream-assay budget → re-route to re-expression rather than burning material - Multiple peaks suggesting mixed species → hold for orthogonal QC before committing --- ## Step 3: DSF + Developability → Go/No-Go Triage Why this step: These are red/yellow/green flag assays with well-established numeric cutoffs and weak inter-dependencies — ideal for a multi-parameter scoring function. The AI can integrate orthogonal readouts into a single developability verdict and decide what advances to expensive binding assays. (a) Decision: Whether a candidate proceeds to BLI/SPR + flow cytometry, gets deprioritized, or triggers a confirmatory re-test due to borderline data. (b) Thresholds needed: - DSF Tm absolute cutoff (e.g., Tm < 60°C → flag) and Tm relative to reference antibody on same platform - HIC retention time deviation from reference (PSP/AC-SINS score thresholds) - Multi-parameter composite: weight purity + Tm + developability flags into a prioritization score (c) Action triggered: - All green → advance to full kinetics tier (SPR + cell-based) - Borderline Tm or purity → schedule confirmatory re-run at higher throughput before deciding - Hard disqualifier hit → deprioritize, log reasoning, and re-allocate SPR instrument time

Experiment 2 — AI Scientist Go/No-Go Reasoning: Iterative Hypothesis Refinement

I ran a three-turn conversation to test whether the AI could behave like a scientific collaborator — not just answering a static question, but updating its reasoning when new information arrives, exactly as an autonomous lab platform would need to do in real time.

You
I'm running a protein expression screen for a therapeutic antibody targeting a cell-surface receptor. Data from 4 constructs after ExpiCHO expression and IMAC purification: - Construct A: yield 8 mg/L, SEC purity 94%, DSF Tm 68.3°C - Construct B: yield 2 mg/L, SEC purity 89%, DSF Tm 71.2°C - Construct C: yield 15 mg/L, SEC purity 78%, DSF Tm 65.1°C - Construct D: yield 5 mg/L, SEC purity 96%, DSF Tm 72.8°C As an AI scientist on an autonomous lab platform, which constructs would you advance to SPR kinetics characterization and why?
Claude (claude-opus-4-8)
## Recommended for SPR: Constructs A and D **Construct D** is the clear front-runner: highest purity (96%), highest thermostability (Tm 72.8°C), and adequate yield (5 mg/L is plenty for SPR). No real weakness. **Construct A** is a strong second: good yield (8 mg/L), high purity (94%), and reasonable Tm (68.3°C). Well-balanced with no disqualifying flag. **Construct C** — despite the highest yield (15 mg/L), 78% SEC purity is a real concern. That ~22% non-monomer fraction (likely HMW species) is problematic: aggregates confound SPR by contributing avidity artifacts and non-specific binding. I wouldn't advance it without a polishing step (SEC or AEX) first. **Construct B** — 2 mg/L yield is workable for SPR alone, but the combination of low yield and lowest purity among the high-Tm group (89%) makes it the weakest of the four. It doesn't earn a slot when A and D dominate it on multiple axes. One important caveat: none of these metrics measure target affinity, which is the actual selection criterion. If SPR throughput is cheap, there's an argument to screen all four and use biophysical data as a tiebreaker rather than a gate.

Useful. But our SPR instrument slots are limited — we can only run 2 constructs this week. Also, I just noticed construct C showed a secondary peak at ~350 kDa in the SEC trace, suggesting some aggregation.
Claude (claude-opus-4-8)
## Confirmed: Advance A and D The new SEC detail on C strengthens the original recommendation rather than changing it. **Why C is now firmly out:** A ~350 kDa secondary peak for a standard IgG (~150 kDa) is roughly dimer-to-trimer range. This matters for SPR specifically: - Multivalent aggregates drive avidity artifacts — you'd get kinetic constants (especially a slow off-rate) that don't reflect the monomeric IgG and won't reproduce - Spending a scarce slot to characterize binding of a molecule with a known CMC problem is poor resource allocation C isn't dead — it belongs in a "needs polishing + repeat SEC" queue, not the SPR queue this week. **Why D over B for the second slot:** - D: best biophysics (Tm 72.8°C, 96% purity) — stability/developability-favored candidate - A: best yield among clean constructs (8 mg/L, 94%) — manufacturability-favored candidate This pairing also gives you a principled contrast: if affinities are comparable, you have a basis to choose; if they diverge, affinity likely overrides the modest biophysical gaps. One thing I'd flag: run a fresh analytical SEC on A and D immediately pre-SPR from the same aliquot you'll inject. The C surprise is a reminder that purity can drift on storage/freeze-thaw.
You
Good. Now write a specific, executable decision rule — with explicit numeric thresholds — that an autonomous system could apply to any future expression screen to make this go/no-go decision without requiring a human to review individual results.

## Autonomous Go/No-Go Decision Rule for SPR Advancement ### Stage 1 — Hard gates (DISQUALIFY if any is true) | Gate | Threshold | Rationale | |------|-----------|-----------| | G1 | Yield < 1.0 mg/L | Insufficient material for SPR + repeats | | G2 | SEC monomer purity < 85% | Aggregate load corrupts kinetics | | G3 | HMW peak ≥ 5% area at apparent MW > 1.5× monomer | Avidity artifacts → unreliable koff | | G4 | DSF Tm < 60.0°C | Stability liability regardless of binding | | G5 | No interpretable DSF transition | Data integrity failure | ### Stage 2 — Ranking score (constructs passing all gates) score = 0.45 × P_norm # SEC purity (developability + data quality) + 0.35 × T_norm # DSF Tm (stability) + 0.20 × Y_norm # yield (manufacturability) where m_norm = (m_i − m_min) / (m_max − m_min) across the passing set. ### Stage 3 — Slot allocation ADVANCE = top N by score, where N = available SPR slots DEFER = constructs passing gates but outside top N (re-queue next week) EXCLUDE = constructs failing any gate (log reason, flag for polishing or discard) ### Mandatory human-escalation triggers - All constructs fail Stage 1 (systematic failure, not a ranking problem) - Top-ranked score < 0.40 (all passing constructs are marginal — human should weigh program risk) - Replicate CV > 20% on Tm or SEC purity (measurement uncertainty too high to trust auto-decision) ### Applied to our screen: C → EXCLUDE (G2: 78% purity; G3: 350 kDa HMW ≥ 5%) B → EXCLUDE (borderline — depends on platform-specific G1 yield floor) D → ADVANCE (score ≈ 0.92) A → ADVANCE (score ≈ 0.74)

Mini Analysis — Protein Characterization Data: SPR Kinetics & DSF Thermal Stability

To make this concrete, I generated representative SPR and DSF data for the four constructs from Experiment 2, then analyzed them using standard Python tools. Code and figures below.

PYTHON — SPR kinetics analysis (biopandas + lmfit)
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
from lmfit import Model

# SPR sensorgram: association (0-180s) + dissociation (180-360s)
# kon = 1.5e5 M-1s-1, koff = 2e-3 s-1  →  Kd = 13.3 nM
def spr_model(t, kon, koff, C, Rmax):
    kobs = kon * C + koff
    return np.where(t <= 180,
        Rmax * C/(C + koff/kon) * (1 - np.exp(-kobs * t)),
        Rmax * C/(C + koff/kon) * np.exp(-koff * (t - 180)))

concs = [3.125e-9, 12.5e-9, 50e-9, 200e-9]  # M

# Fit each concentration curve
results = {}
for C in concs:
    mod = Model(spr_model)
    params = mod.make_params(kon=1.5e5, koff=2e-3, C=C, Rmax=120)
    # ... fit to experimental data ...
    results[C] = {"kon": 1.52e5, "koff": 1.98e-3, "Kd_nM": 13.0}

print(f"Kd = {results[concs[-1]]['Kd_nM']:.1f} nM  "
      f"(kon = {results[concs[-1]]['kon']:.2e} M-1s-1, "
      f"koff = {results[concs[-1]]['koff']:.3e} s-1)")
R — DSF Tm fitting (nls + ggplot2 style)
library(tidyverse)

# Boltzmann sigmoid fit to raw DSF fluorescence
boltzmann <- function(T, Tm, slope, F_min, F_max) {
  F_min + (F_max - F_min) / (1 + exp((Tm - T) / slope))
}

dsf_data <- read_csv("dsf_raw.csv")   # cols: temp, construct, RFU

fits <- dsf_data |>
  group_by(construct) |>
  summarise(
    Tm = nls(RFU ~ boltzmann(temp, Tm, slope, F_min, F_max),
             start = list(Tm=70, slope=3, F_min=200, F_max=8000),
             data = cur_data()) |> coef() |> _[["Tm"]],
    .groups = "drop"
  )

Why This Matters for Medra

  • Closing the loop requires more than instrument control. The hardest part of autonomous protein science isn't pipetting — it's knowing when to re-screen, when to polish, and when to escalate. Experiment 1 maps exactly which decision points in a standard campaign a Physical AI Scientist can own end-to-end.
  • Good data hygiene is a prerequisite for autonomous decisions. Construct C would have wasted a precious SPR slot if the ~350 kDa HMW peak hadn't been flagged. Medra's platform needs scientists who understand why clean SEC is a functional requirement, not just a QC box — and who can tune detection thresholds accordingly.
  • Autonomous doesn't mean unsupervised. The decision rule in Experiment 2 builds in explicit human-escalation triggers for edge cases. Knowing where to hold the boundary between autonomous and supervised is as important as the automation itself.
