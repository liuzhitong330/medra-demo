Medra is building an autonomous platform that runs protein characterization end to end, the kind of biophysical panel a Research Associate sets up every day: expression titer, thermal stability by DSF, hydrophobicity by HIC, self-interaction by AC-SINS and BLI, and non-specificity by PSR. The hard part is not running the assays, it is reading twelve numbers at once and deciding whether a molecule is developable or should be dropped.

I built a small tool over a landmark reference set so you can look up any clinical-stage antibody and see its full biophysical profile against the pack, with the same red flags a triage step would raise.

How it works

Every antibody here was expressed and put through the same twelve assays. Type an antibody below and the chart places it on each assay relative to all the others, with the worst tenth of molecules marked as a red flag. A clean molecule sits near the middle of every row; a risky one drifts into the flagged zone on several.

Result
Developability burden falls as antibodies reach approval
share of antibodies carrying two or more red flags, by clinical stage · click a bar for an example

The antibodies that reached approval are the cleanest of the set. They average about 0.75 red flags, and only around a quarter carry two or more, against nearly half of the phase-2 candidates. The same cheap bench assays that a Research Associate runs in a day front-load a signal about which molecules will behave, years before the clinic settles it. That is the case for building the panel into an autonomous platform: it turns a slow, expensive verdict into a same-day one.

Why this matters for Medra

An AI scientist that closes the loop has to turn raw assay output into a call: advance, repeat, or drop. This tool is a small version of that reasoning layer, anchored on a real reference set, and it speaks the same language as the characterization pipeline in the role, HIC, AC-SINS, PSR, DSF, BLI. I am comfortable both at the bench generating this data and in code turning it into a decision.

Methods note

Data: Jain et al., PNAS 2017 (Supplementary Dataset), 137 clinical-stage antibodies expressed as matched IgG1 and evaluated across 12 biophysical assays. For each assay a molecule is placed on the observed range of all antibodies, and flagged if it falls in the worst 10% in the unfavourable direction (for example high HIC retention, high AC-SINS, or low Fab Tm). The flag threshold is a transparent per-assay percentile chosen here for illustration, not the exact cutoffs from the paper, and some assays have missing values for a few molecules.
